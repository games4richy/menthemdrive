Should You Buy a Car Now or Wait? How Tariffs Are Changing Prices in 2026

By Marcus Adeyemi·September 13, 2026·11 min read

Car prices were already high before tariffs entered the picture. The average new vehicle now transacts somewhere around $49,000 to $52,000, depending on which month and which tracker you look at. Tariffs on imported vehicles and parts have added to that, and the pressure has spread into the used market too.

That leaves a lot of shoppers asking a simple question: buy now, or wait for things to settle? The honest answer depends on what you drive today, what you want next, and where that vehicle is built. Here's how the costs actually flow through to the sticker, and how to decide.

How a Tariff Reaches the Window Sticker

A tariff is a tax on imported goods, paid by the importer, not by the exporting country. For cars, it can apply in two ways: to fully built vehicles shipped in from abroad, and to parts that cross the border before a car is finished.

The second one surprises people. Even a vehicle assembled in the United States is full of imported content: transmissions, wiring harnesses, electronics, steel and aluminum. So a tariff can raise the cost of a US-built car as well as an imported one, just usually by less.

Automakers don't pass every dollar straight to buyers. They absorb some of it, spread it across a lineup, trim discounts, drop slow-selling trims, or quietly raise destination charges. That's why the effect shows up less as a dramatic price jump on one model and more as a steady erosion of the deals you used to find.

What the Price Data Shows

Industry trackers have reported new-vehicle prices climbing through 2025 and into 2026, with average transaction prices sitting near or above $49,000 for much of the year and sticker prices frequently topping $50,000. Analysts have attributed part of that to tariff-related costs, with estimates for affected models commonly in the low thousands of dollars.

Two other things push the average up, and they're easy to confuse with tariffs. Buyers keep shifting toward trucks and SUVs, which cost more than sedans, and new safety and tech features keep getting added as standard. Both raise the average even when individual models don't get much more expensive.

The practical takeaway is that the effect isn't uniform. Some models are barely touched. Others, especially those built abroad or heavily dependent on imported parts, have seen larger increases or fewer incentives.

The Knock-On Effect in the Used Market

When new cars get more expensive, some buyers give up and shop used, which increases demand and firms up used prices. Late-model used vehicles, the two-to-four-year-old cars that are usually the sweet spot, tend to feel it first.

That doesn't make used cars a bad idea. It does mean the savings compared with new may be smaller than you remember, and it makes shopping carefully more important. Our used car buying guide covers the checks that protect you when prices are firm and inventory moves quickly.

When Buying Now Makes Sense

Waiting only pays if prices actually fall, and there's no guarantee of that. Buying now usually makes more sense if:

  • Your current car is unreliable or unsafe. Repair bills and breakdowns cost more than a few percent of price movement.
  • You've found a model that's assembled in the US and still discounted. These exist, and they may qualify for the car loan interest deduction.
  • There's a manufacturer incentive on the table. Low-rate financing is worth real money. On a $40,000 loan, dropping from 7% to 2.9% saves thousands over five years, often more than you'd gain by waiting for a small price cut.
  • You're buying a leftover model year. Outgoing inventory still gets discounted, tariffs or not.

When Waiting Makes Sense

Waiting is reasonable if your current car is fine and you're shopping mostly because you want something new. It also makes sense if the model you want is imported and currently carries thin discounts, or if you can be flexible about which model you buy. Trade policy can change quickly, and legal challenges and negotiations have shifted tariff levels more than once, so patience has some option value.

One thing to avoid is waiting in the hope that prices return to where they were a few years ago. Nothing in the current data points that way. Interest rates, incentives and inventory move far more than sticker prices do, and those are usually what decide your monthly payment anyway.

How to Check Whether a Specific Model Is Affected

Start with where it's built. The window sticker lists a final assembly point, and you can also check the plant and country using the NHTSA VIN decoder. The sticker also shows US and Canadian parts content, which hints at how exposed a model is to parts tariffs.

Then compare prices over time. Pricing tools from Edmunds, Kelley Blue Book and CarGurus show what people in your area are actually paying, not just asking. If a model's transaction prices have crept up while incentives have disappeared, that's your answer. If it's still selling below sticker with factory cash on the hood, tariffs clearly aren't dictating that car's price.

Compare payments, not just prices

A $1,500 price increase on a $40,000 car adds about $25 a month over five years. A 2-point difference in interest rate on the same loan is worth roughly double that. When you're weighing buying now against waiting, compare total financed cost, including the rate and any manufacturer incentives, rather than the sticker alone.

Negotiating When Discounts Are Thinner

Tighter pricing changes how you negotiate, but it doesn't make negotiating pointless. Dealer costs still vary, inventory still ages, and quarterly targets still exist. What changes is where the savings come from: fewer big discounts off sticker, more value in financing rates, trade-in values and add-on fees you can decline.

Getting competing out-the-door quotes by email still works, and it works better when supply is tight, because it shows you instantly which dealers have stock they want to move. The full approach is in our car price negotiation guide, and timing still matters too, as we cover in the best time to buy guide.

Does Leasing Help?

Sometimes. Lease payments depend on the difference between the price and the car's predicted resale value, so when used values hold up, residuals stay strong and lease payments can look attractive compared with financing. Automakers also use subsidized lease deals to move specific models.

The catch is that you're renting the car. If prices stay high, the car you hand back in three years will be replaced at whatever the market looks like then. Leasing can be a sensible way to avoid a large purchase in an uncertain market, but run the numbers rather than assuming it's cheaper. Our lease versus buy analysis walks through the math.

Which Vehicles Have Been Hit Hardest

The effect isn't spread evenly across the market, and knowing where it lands helps you shop.

Imported small and mid-size cars have been squeezed most. They were already the thinnest-margin vehicles on sale, so there was little room to absorb extra cost. Several affordable models have been quietly dropped from the US market in recent years, and tariffs give automakers another reason to prioritize expensive trucks and SUVs over cheap sedans.

Luxury and performance models built abroad have seen some of the largest dollar increases, though buyers in that segment tend to be less price-sensitive.

US-assembled trucks and SUVs have generally fared best, though they still use imported parts, so they aren't immune.

The practical consequence for budget shoppers is that the cheapest end of the new market keeps thinning out. If you're looking to spend under about $25,000 on a new car, your choices are far narrower than they were five years ago, which pushes more buyers toward used vehicles and keeps used prices firm.

A Simple Way to Decide

Rather than trying to forecast trade policy, answer four questions:

  1. Does your current car still do the job safely? If no, buy. Waiting to save a few hundred dollars while spending more on repairs is a bad trade.
  2. Is the model you want built in the US, and is it still being discounted? If yes, the tariff argument for waiting is weak.
  3. Can you get a subsidized finance rate right now? Manufacturer rates below about 4% are worth more than most realistic price drops.
  4. Are you flexible on model? If you can switch to a similar vehicle that's less exposed, you can often sidestep the increase entirely.

Two or more yes answers usually means buy now. Mostly no answers means you can afford to wait and watch.

If You're Shopping Used Instead

Used prices respond to new car prices with a lag, so this is a market where patience and flexibility pay. A few tactics help more than usual right now.

Widen your search radius. Prices for the same car vary meaningfully between metro areas, and many dealers will now handle paperwork remotely. Consider slightly less popular models, since the most in-demand nameplates carry the biggest premiums. And be willing to take a car with average rather than very low mileage, because the price gap is often bigger than the difference in remaining life.

Above all, don't let a tight market push you into skipping the checks. A pre-purchase inspection matters more, not less, when prices are high and cars move quickly.

The Bottom Line

Tariffs have made new cars somewhat more expensive, and the effect varies a lot by model. If you need a car, buy the right one at the best financed price you can negotiate. If you don't, waiting costs you nothing, as long as you're waiting for a reason rather than for prices to return to a level they're unlikely to reach again.

Either way, the biggest savings still come from the things you control: which model you choose, where it's built, what rate you finance at, and how many dealers you make compete for your business.

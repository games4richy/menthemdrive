Car Lease vs Buy in 2026: Complete Analysis of Which Option Saves You More

By Marcus Adeyemi·14 min read

Most financial advice treats leasing versus buying as simple: leasing always costs more in the long run, so buying is always smarter. That's wrong often enough to deserve a closer look. Leasing isn't always more expensive, and sometimes it's exactly the right call. People who say it's always a mistake often make the same error as people who say it's always a great deal: they look at part of the picture and hand down a verdict based on incomplete math.

What matters is how the numbers work out for you, not someone else's conclusion applied to your situation.

How Leasing Works: The Math Behind the Payment

A car lease is a contract where you pay for using a car and for the value it loses over a set period, usually 24, 36, or 39 months, without owning it at the end. You pay for the part of the car you use up, not for what's left.

It's worth understanding how the monthly payment is calculated, because it shows where your money goes and where dealers have room to pad the numbers. A lease payment has two parts: depreciation and a finance charge.

The depreciation part is the capitalized cost minus the residual value, divided by the number of months. The capitalized cost is the negotiated price of the car, plus any fees or negative equity rolled in. The residual value is what the leasing company predicts the car will be worth at the end of the lease. It's set as a percentage of MSRP by the automaker's finance company, not by you or the dealer.

The finance charge is the capitalized cost plus the residual value, multiplied by the money factor. This is where most people's eyes glaze over, and it's where dealers take advantage of the confusion.

Here's an example with realistic numbers. Say you're leasing a 2026 Honda CR-V Sport Hybrid with an MSRP of about $35,600. After negotiating, the capitalized cost is $34,300. The lease runs 36 months. The residual is 58% of MSRP, or $20,648. The money factor is 0.00175, which we'll translate into a familiar interest rate in a moment.

Monthly depreciation: ($34,300 − $20,648) ÷ 36 = $379.22. Monthly finance charge: ($34,300 + $20,648) × 0.00175 = $54,948 × 0.00175 = $96.16. Total monthly payment before tax and fees: $379.22 + $96.16 = about $475.

Money Factor: The Number Dealers Don't Volunteer

The money factor is a lease's version of an interest rate, written in a format that makes it nearly impossible to compare with a loan rate unless you convert it. To turn it into an approximate APR, multiply by 2,400. Our money factor of 0.00175 × 2,400 = 4.2% APR. Now you can compare the lease's financing cost directly with a car loan.

If you could get a purchase loan at 5.5% and the lease works out to 4.2%, the lease is cheaper on financing. If your loan rate is 3% and the lease works out to 6%, the loan is cheaper. It's a simple calculation, and it's worth doing before you sign any lease.

Here's one way dealers make extra money. They're often allowed to mark up the money factor above the "buy rate," the rate the automaker's finance company actually offers, by a certain amount, commonly up to about 0.0004 to 0.0008. That sounds tiny, but it adds roughly 1 to 2 percentage points to the effective rate. In our example, a 0.0005 markup adds about $27 a month, or nearly $1,000 over a 36-month lease, straight to the dealer's profit, and it won't be pointed out to you. Ask the dealer to confirm the buy-rate money factor, and check it against the current published rates for that automaker. Forums like Leasehackr regularly share this data for mainstream brands.

Residual Value: The Core of Every Lease Deal

The residual value is the most important number in a lease, and neither you nor the dealer controls it. The automaker's finance company sets it based on how much it expects the car to lose in value. A high residual is good for you, since you pay for less depreciation. A low residual works against you.

Residuals run high when an automaker wants to boost leasing on a model. It effectively subsidizes the lease by assuming the car will hold more value than the market suggests. Honda and Toyota sometimes do this on specific models to move inventory. These are real lease deals where the payment is lower than the car's actual depreciation would justify. If you see an automaker advertising a very low monthly payment on a model known for strong resale value, a boosted residual is often why.

Residuals run low on cars that lose value quickly, such as many luxury brands and some EVs, on models the automaker doesn't want to push through leasing, or when finance companies are correcting after overestimating values in the past. Leasing a car with a low residual is expensive, because you're paying for more depreciation every month.

So before you commit, check the residual for the exact model, trim, and term. Leasehackr and similar forums are good sources, or ask the dealer to show it on the lease worksheet. For a mainstream car on a 36-month lease, a residual above about 55% is generally favorable. Below about 45% is a warning sign that the car is losing value fast and your payment reflects it. If the residual is 40%, you're paying for 60% of the car's value in three years, which is almost always more expensive than buying and selling.

True Total Cost of a 3-Year Lease

Most people compare monthly lease payments with monthly loan payments. That consistently understates what leasing really costs, because it ignores what you're left with at the end. Here's the full picture for our CR-V Sport Hybrid example.

Up front: an acquisition fee, typically $595 to $895 (we'll use $750), plus a modest cash down payment or drive-off amount, say about one month's payment of $475. That's roughly $1,225 to start the lease. Some leases also require the first month's payment at signing, which we've counted in the monthly payments below.

Over 36 months: $475 × 36 = about $17,110 in monthly payments.

At the end: you hand back the car and own nothing. You might also face excess mileage charges if you've gone over your limit (usually $0.20 to $0.25 per mile), charges for wear beyond "normal," and a disposition fee, often $300 to $400, if you don't lease another car from the same brand.

Total cost for three years of driving: about $1,225 up front + $17,110 in payments + a $350 disposition fee = roughly $18,690. After 36 months you have no car, and you'll need to lease again, buy, or find another option.

The Buying Math: Same Vehicle, Same Period

To compare fairly, let's work out what it costs to buy the same CR-V Sport Hybrid, keep it for the same 36 months, and then sell it.

Negotiated price: $34,300. Down payment: $3,000. Amount financed: $31,300 at 6.5% APR over 60 months, which works out to about $612 a month. After 36 months you've paid $3,000 down plus $612 × 36, or $22,030, for a total of about $25,030. The remaining loan balance at that point is about $13,750.

What's the car worth after three years and about 36,000 miles? Popular hybrid SUVs like the CR-V hold their value well. We'll assume it's worth about 62% of MSRP, or roughly $22,000. Your equity: $22,000 value − $13,750 owed = about $8,250.

Net cost of owning it for 36 months: $25,030 paid − $8,250 in equity = about $16,780. Compared with the lease's $18,690, buying costs roughly $1,900 less over the same three years. And the buyer walks away with more than savings. That $8,250 in equity is real money toward the next car, or they can simply keep driving a car they partly own. The person leasing starts again from zero.

One more thing about the lease in this example. If the CR-V is worth $22,000 at the end but the lease lets you buy it for the $20,648 residual, you could buy it out and capture that difference. When residuals turn out lower than market value, the lease buyout option can be worth a lot.

The 9-Year Comparison: Three Lease Cycles vs Two Buy Cycles

The fairest long-term comparison covers the same stretch of time. Nine years equals three 36-month leases, or roughly two cycles of buying a car and keeping it for four or five years. Here's how leasing three times compares with buying twice over about nine years.

Three 36-month leases of similar cars, with payments creeping up a little each time, add up to roughly $55,000 to $65,000 over nine years. At the end, you're driving a leased 2035 SUV and own nothing, with about $60,000 spent.

Buying twice, first a 2026 CR-V Sport Hybrid paid off over five years, then a newer SUV partly funded by the trade-in, adds up to roughly $50,000 to $58,000 in payments over nine years. At the end you own a car worth something like $14,000 to $18,000, and you likely enjoyed several years with no car payment after paying off the first loan. Those payment-free years are the biggest financial advantage of buying and holding. They're the months you'd otherwise still be sending lease payments.

Over nine years, leasing typically costs $5,000 to $10,000 more in payments, and you have no car to show for it, while the buyer has equity. That's the real long-term financial case for buying: all those months of driving a paid-off car while someone who always leases keeps making payments.

Why EV Leasing Is a Special Case in 2026

EV leasing used to have a big built-in advantage. Until September 30, 2025, leasing companies could claim a $7,500 federal commercial clean vehicle credit on leased EVs and often passed it on as a lower price, even to high-income drivers who couldn't get the consumer credit when buying. That credit ended along with the other federal EV tax credits, so 2026 lessees no longer get that boost.

EV leasing can still make sense for other reasons. Many automakers now use subsidized lease deals, with boosted residuals, low money factors, or cash incentives, to keep EVs affordable after losing the tax credit, and some EV lease offers are very aggressive. EVs have also had volatile resale values and fast-changing technology, with range, charging speeds, and software improving quickly. Leasing lets you hand the resale risk to the leasing company and move to a newer EV in a few years if the technology leaps ahead. For EVs specifically, being able to walk away at the end of the lease can be worth more than it is for gas cars, which change more slowly.

When Buying Clearly Wins

Buying costs less overall when:

  • You drive more than about 12,000 to 15,000 miles a year. Standard leases usually allow 10,000 to 12,000 miles a year, and overage charges of $0.20 to $0.25 per mile add up fast.
  • You keep your cars more than five years, especially once you reach those payment-free years after the loan is paid off.
  • You want to modify the car.
  • You tow or haul regularly in ways that cause more than normal wear.
  • You want the freedom to sell or trade the car whenever you like, without early termination penalties.

That last point is one of the biggest practical differences between the two. When you own a car, you can sell it, trade it, or pay off the loan at any time, paying only normal transaction costs. A lease locks you in for the full term, and ending one early usually costs thousands and is hard to do on reasonable terms. If your life is unpredictable, with a possible job move, a growing family, or uncertain finances, that lack of flexibility is a real risk.

When Leasing Makes Legitimate Financial Sense

Leasing can cost less or offer real advantages when:

  • You drive fewer than about 10,000 miles a year, so you'll stay well within the mileage limits.
  • You use the car mainly for business. The tax treatment of lease payments can sometimes be more favorable than depreciation on a car you own. Talk to your accountant.
  • The automaker is offering a subsidized lease with a boosted residual or low money factor, so the payment is lower than the car's real depreciation.
  • You always want to drive a new car under full warranty and don't want to deal with repair costs on an aging one.
  • You're leasing an EV with a strong manufacturer lease incentive and want to avoid the resale risk and fast-changing technology.

The Mileage Trap: The Most Common Lease Mistake

The most common leasing mistake is lowballing your yearly mileage at signing to get a smaller payment, then getting hit with excess mileage charges at the end that wipe out everything you saved.

Here's how it plays out. A 36-month lease with 10,000 miles a year might cost $30 a month less than the same lease with 15,000 miles a year, which saves $1,080 over the lease. But say you actually drive 14,000 miles a year. That's 12,000 miles over the limit, and at $0.25 a mile you owe $3,000 when you turn the car in. You "saved" $1,080 on payments and paid $3,000 at the end, a net loss of $1,920 compared with just choosing the right mileage from the start.

Estimate your real yearly mileage honestly, then add 10 to 15% as a cushion. Buying extra miles up front, which usually works out to about $0.08 to $0.15 per mile built into a higher-mileage lease, is much cheaper than paying $0.20 to $0.25 per mile in overage fees at the end. Paying for miles at turn-in almost always costs more than buying them at signing.

F&I Office Lease Traps

The finance and insurance (F&I) office has a few lease-specific ways to squeeze out extra profit. Know them before you sit down.

Extra gap insurance. Many leases from automakers' finance companies already include gap coverage, which protects you if the car is totaled and you owe more than it's worth. Check your lease contract. If gap coverage is already included and the finance manager tries to sell you more, say no. You'd be paying for coverage you already have.

Padded fees. The automaker's acquisition fee is a normal, legitimate charge. But some dealers quietly add their own extra fees to the capitalized cost. Ask directly whether the quoted capitalized cost includes any dealer-added fees beyond the standard acquisition fee, and check every line of the lease worksheet.

Extended warranties. Most leases run no longer than the car's factory bumper-to-bumper warranty, so an extended warranty rarely makes sense. If your lease is shorter than the factory coverage, as most three-year leases are, turn them down.

End of Lease: Your Three Options and Which Is Usually Best

When your lease ends, you have three choices. Knowing them lets you plan 6 to 12 months ahead instead of deciding in a rush at the last minute.

Option 1: Return the car and lease a new one. This is the default, and what the automaker's finance company wants. You get a new car, a fresh warranty, and the latest tech. The costs are a disposition fee, which is often waived if you lease another car from the same brand, and starting the depreciation and payment cycle all over again.

Option 2: Buy the car for its residual value. This makes sense when the car is worth more on the open market than the residual price in your lease. You can buy it for less than it's worth, then keep it or sell it and pocket the difference. If the car is worth less than the residual, you can simply hand it back and let the leasing company take the loss. Popular trucks and SUVs have sometimes been worth more than their residuals, so it's always worth checking your car's market value before turning it in.

Option 3: Return it and buy or lease something different. This gives you the most freedom but takes planning. If you're switching to a different car, start shopping 60 to 90 days before your lease ends, so you're not pressured into a bad deal by your turn-in date.

The Honest Verdict

For most people who drive an average 12,000 to 15,000 miles a year and keep their cars five years or more, buying beats leasing on total cost over time, mainly because of the payment-free years after the loan is paid off, which people who always lease never get. The advantage isn't as big as the "leasing is always a rip-off" crowd claims, but over a long ownership period it's real.

If you drive fewer than 10,000 miles a year, always want a new car under warranty, use the car for business with meaningful tax benefits, or can get a heavily subsidized lease deal, especially on an EV, leasing can be the right financial choice. Anyone who says otherwise is using the wrong framework for your situation.

The worst outcomes come from borrowing someone else's conclusion without running the numbers for your own cars, mileage, driving habits, and finances. Do the math using the formulas in this article. The right answer for you will be much clearer than any rule of thumb.

The fastest way to decide

If you drive more than 12,000 miles a year, buy. If you drive less than 10,000 miles a year and always want a new car, lease. If you're between 10,000 and 12,000 miles, run the numbers for the exact cars you're considering, including the money factor conversion, the residual value, and the total cost over how long you plan to keep the car. It takes about 30 minutes and is worth far more than any rule of thumb.

Car Loan Interest Tax Deduction 2026: Who Qualifies and How Much You Save

By Marcus Adeyemi·September 13, 2026·11 min read

For the first time in decades, the interest you pay on a car loan can lower your federal tax bill. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed in July 2025, created a deduction of up to $10,000 a year for interest on loans used to buy a new, personal-use vehicle assembled in the United States. It runs for tax years 2025 through 2028.

It's a genuinely useful break, and it's also easy to misunderstand. It's a deduction, not a credit, so it reduces the income you're taxed on rather than cutting your tax bill dollar for dollar. Plenty of cars don't qualify, and the benefit shrinks and then disappears as your income rises. Here's how it works, who it helps, and what it's actually worth.

What the Deduction Actually Does

A deduction lowers your taxable income. If you deduct $2,500 of car loan interest and you're in the 22% federal bracket, you save about $550 in tax, not $2,500. That's still real money, but it's much smaller than the headline number suggests.

The unusual part is that you don't have to itemize to claim it. Most people take the standard deduction and never itemize, and this break sits outside that choice, so you can claim it either way. Historically, only business owners could write off car loan interest at all, which makes this a meaningful change for ordinary buyers.

It's also temporary. As written, it applies to tax years 2025 through 2028. Unless Congress extends it, interest paid in later years won't qualify.

Who Qualifies, and the Income Limits

The deduction starts to shrink once your modified adjusted gross income passes $100,000 if you file single, or $200,000 if you file jointly. Above those points, it drops by $200 for every $1,000 of income. That means it phases out completely at $150,000 for single filers and $250,000 for joint filers.

So a single filer earning $120,000 has gone $20,000 past the threshold and loses $4,000 of the deduction. Someone earning $150,000 or more gets nothing. If your income sits near the line, it's worth checking where you'll land before counting on the money.

Which Vehicles Count

The vehicle rules are stricter than most people expect. To qualify, the vehicle must be:

  • New, not used. Used vehicles don't qualify at all, no matter how new they look or how recently they were sold.
  • Assembled in the United States. Final assembly must have happened in the US. That covers roughly half the vehicles sold here, including plenty from brands most people think of as imports.
  • For personal use. Vehicles bought for business or commercial use, or for a fleet, are excluded.
  • Under 14,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating. That covers cars, minivans, vans, SUVs, pickups and motorcycles.
  • Secured by the loan itself. The lender has to hold a lien on the vehicle, which is how nearly all car loans work.

The loan also has to have started after December 31, 2024. Older loans don't qualify, even if you're still paying interest on them.

What Doesn't Qualify

A few common situations fall outside the rules, and they catch people out:

  • Leases. A lease isn't a loan secured by the vehicle, so lease payments don't qualify.
  • Used and certified pre-owned cars. Even a one-year-old CPO car is used for these purposes.
  • Imported vehicles. If final assembly happened outside the US, the loan doesn't qualify, whatever the badge on the hood says.
  • Personal loans or home equity loans used to buy a car, because they aren't secured by the vehicle.
  • Business vehicles. Those follow separate, long-standing rules for business expenses.

Refinancing is a common question. In general, refinanced loans can still qualify, but only up to the amount of the original qualifying loan, and other conditions apply. Because refinancing can get complicated, check the current guidance or talk to a tax preparer before you assume you're covered.

How to Check Where a Car Was Assembled

Don't guess, and don't rely on the brand's nationality. Many Japanese, Korean and German brands build best-sellers in American factories, and plenty of American brands build popular models in Mexico or Canada.

There are two reliable ways to check. The window sticker, officially the Monroney label, lists a "final assembly point" on every new vehicle. You can also run the VIN through the federal NHTSA VIN decoder, which reports the plant and country where the vehicle was built. Do this before you sign anything, because two versions of the same model can be built in different countries.

How Much Is It Really Worth?

Car loan interest is front-loaded, so the deduction is worth the most in the first couple of years, then tapers off as your balance falls.

Take a fairly typical example: $45,000 financed at 7% over 72 months. In the first full year you'd pay roughly $3,000 in interest. If you're in the 22% bracket and under the income limits, that's about $660 off your tax bill. By year four, the annual interest is closer to $1,500, worth about $330. Over the life of that loan, and within the 2025 to 2028 window, you might save somewhere around $1,500 to $1,800 in total.

A smaller loan means a smaller deduction. Borrow $25,000 at 7% and you'd pay about $1,650 in first-year interest, worth roughly $360 at the 22% rate. The $10,000 annual cap only comes into play on very large or very high-rate loans, so most buyers will deduct far less than the maximum.

How to Claim It

You'll need two things: the amount of qualifying interest you paid, and the vehicle's VIN, which has to be reported on your return. Lenders report the interest they collect, so the figure should be documented rather than something you estimate.

If you use tax software, expect it to ask whether you bought a new vehicle and to request the VIN. Keep your loan documents, the purchase agreement and the window sticker together, since the sticker is your easiest proof of where the car was built. For the official rules, see the IRS page on the car loan interest deduction.

Don't let the tax tail wag the dog

A deduction worth a few hundred dollars a year should not decide which car you buy, and it certainly shouldn't push you into borrowing more than you planned. Paying an extra $3,000 in interest to save $660 in tax leaves you worse off. Choose the car and the loan that make sense, then claim the deduction if you qualify.

Should It Change What You Buy?

Only at the margins. If you're already deciding between two similar vehicles and one was assembled in the US, the deduction is a fair tiebreaker. It might also narrow the gap between a new car and a late-model used one, since only new cars qualify. Even so, a used car's lower price and slower depreciation usually outweigh a modest tax break, as we cover in our new versus used comparison.

What the deduction should never do is talk you into a bigger loan. The interest you deduct is interest you paid first. A lower rate, a bigger down payment or a shorter term will save you more than the deduction ever returns.

State Taxes Are a Separate Question

This is a federal deduction. States set their own rules, and many don't follow federal changes automatically. Some may allow a similar deduction, some won't, and others may take time to decide. If your state has an income tax, check with your state revenue department or your tax preparer rather than assuming the same break applies.

Two Buyers, Two Very Different Outcomes

Take two people who each buy a new SUV in 2026 for about $45,000.

Buyer A earns $70,000, finances $40,000 at 7% over 72 months, and chooses a model built in Tennessee. First-year interest is roughly $2,700. She's under the income limits, so she deducts all of it. In the 22% bracket, that's about $594 back.

Buyer B earns $140,000 and buys a similar SUV assembled in Mexico. He gets nothing at all, for two separate reasons: the vehicle fails the final assembly test, and his income has already cut the available deduction to a fraction of the full amount. Same price, same loan, completely different tax outcome.

The lesson isn't that Buyer B chose badly. It's that the deduction rewards a specific combination of circumstances, and it's worth knowing where you stand before you assume it applies to you.

Questions People Keep Asking

Can I claim it on more than one vehicle? The $10,000 cap applies to your total qualifying interest for the year, not per vehicle. If a household finances two qualifying cars, the interest counts toward the same annual limit.

What if my spouse and I file separately? The income thresholds and treatment differ by filing status, and separate filing often works out worse. If you're close to the limits, it's worth modelling both ways before filing.

Does a trade-in change anything? No. A trade-in lowers the amount you finance, which lowers your interest, which lowers your deduction. That's still a good outcome, because paying less interest beats deducting more of it.

What if I bought in 2025? The deduction covers tax years 2025 through 2028, so a qualifying loan taken out in 2025 counts. If you filed without claiming it, ask your tax preparer whether amending makes sense.

Does the dealer handle this for me? No. Unlike the old EV credit, which could be applied at the point of sale, this one is claimed on your tax return. The dealer's job is simply to sell you the car.

What to Keep

Put four things in one folder the day you buy:

  • The purchase agreement showing the vehicle is new and bought for personal use
  • The window sticker, which names the final assembly point
  • The loan agreement, showing the lender holds a lien on the vehicle
  • The VIN, which you'll need to enter on your tax return

Your lender will report the interest you paid, and you'll get a statement showing the annual total. Keep those statements each year for as long as the deduction runs.

The Bottom Line

If you bought a new, US-assembled vehicle for personal use with a loan taken out after 2024, and your income is under the phase-out range, you should be claiming this deduction. For most buyers it's worth a few hundred dollars a year, and it takes only a VIN and an interest total to claim.

Just keep it in proportion. It's a helpful discount on a cost you were already paying, not a reason to buy a car you don't need. Get the price right first, using the email negotiation method and the best loan rate you can find, and treat the tax saving as a bonus.

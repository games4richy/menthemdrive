What Is an EREV? The Extended-Range Electric Trucks and SUVs Coming Next

By Marcus Adeyemi·September 13, 2026·10 min read

A few years ago, the industry's plan looked simple: replace gas engines with batteries. That plan has been rewritten. Ford cancelled the electric F-150 Lightning as we knew it, Ram shelved a fully electric pickup, and both replaced them with something different: extended-range electric vehicles, or EREVs.

The pitch is appealing. You get an electric car for daily driving and a small gas engine on board that acts as a generator when the battery runs low, so a long trip doesn't depend on finding a working fast charger. Here's how EREVs actually work, how they differ from the hybrids you already know, and which ones are coming.

How an EREV Works

An EREV is an electric vehicle with a backup power source. Electric motors drive the wheels all the time. There's a usable battery, typically good for somewhere between 100 and 150 miles, that you charge by plugging in. When that battery runs low, a gas engine starts up and works purely as a generator, producing electricity to keep you moving.

The key difference from a normal hybrid is that the engine usually has no mechanical connection to the wheels. It doesn't shift gears or drive the car. It just makes electricity. That's why an EREV still feels like an EV when the gas engine is running: the throttle response, the quiet at low speeds, and the smooth power delivery all come from the electric motors.

EREV vs Hybrid vs Plug-In Hybrid vs EV

The categories blur together in advertising, so here's the plain version:

  • Hybrid (like a RAV4 Hybrid): small battery, no plug, gas engine drives the wheels most of the time with electric assist. You just buy gas.
  • Plug-in hybrid (like a Tucson Plug-in Hybrid): bigger battery, usually 25 to 40 miles of electric range, and the gas engine can drive the wheels directly once the battery is depleted.
  • EREV: much bigger battery, typically 100 to 150 miles of electric range, and the engine generally only generates electricity rather than driving the wheels.
  • EV: battery only. No engine, no gas, and range depends entirely on charging.

Nissan's e-Power system is a useful comparison. It uses the same generator idea, but with a small battery and no plug, so you can't run it on cheap home electricity. An EREV is essentially that concept scaled up, with a battery big enough to cover most people's daily driving.

Why Automakers Pivoted to EREVs

Three practical problems pushed this change, and all three are worst in big trucks and SUVs.

Towing destroys electric range. Pulling a heavy trailer can cut an EV's range by half or more. A truck rated for 300 miles empty might manage 130 while towing, which turns a long haul into a series of charging stops, many of which aren't designed for a vehicle with a trailer attached.

Batteries are heavy and expensive. Giving a full-size electric truck genuinely long range means an enormous battery, which adds weight, cost and cargo penalties. An EREV can use a battery roughly a third the size and make up the difference with a fuel tank.

Charging isn't evenly distributed. Public charging has improved a lot, as we cover in our guide to home versus public charging, but coverage still thins out on rural routes, exactly where work trucks often go.

The EREVs Coming to America

Ram 1500 REV (the truck first shown as the Ramcharger). Ram's extended-range pickup pairs a battery good for around 145 miles of electric driving with a V6 generator, for a total range of roughly 690 miles. It's expected to reach production in the second half of 2026 as a 2027 model.

Scout Terra and Traveler. Scout Motors, revived under Volkswagen Group, will offer its pickup and SUV with an optional gas generator it calls Harvester. The company has talked about roughly 150 miles of electric range and prices starting under $60,000, with production at a new South Carolina plant.

Ford F-150 Lightning EREV. After ending production of the original electric Lightning in December 2025, Ford said its replacement will use an extended-range setup with a targeted total range of more than 700 miles. Ford hasn't confirmed full pricing or an on-sale date.

Several other brands have announced EREV plans, and more will follow if these sell. Treat announced range figures as manufacturer targets until independent testing confirms them.

The Honest Pros and Cons

Strengths

  • Most daily driving runs on electricity if you charge at home
  • No range anxiety on long trips or in remote areas
  • Towing doesn't strand you, because the generator keeps working
  • Smaller battery than a comparable EV, which helps cost and weight
  • Onboard power for tools and camping, like other electric trucks

Weaknesses

  • You still maintain an engine: oil, filters, coolant and fuel system
  • Efficiency drops once the battery is depleted and the engine is running
  • More complex than either an EV or a regular hybrid
  • Little long-term reliability data, since these are all new systems
  • Prices are expected to be high, mostly in truck and large SUV territory

Who an EREV Actually Suits

The strongest case is a driver who can charge at home, does most daily miles locally, and occasionally needs to tow or drive long distances where charging is inconvenient. That's a lot of truck owners. You'd run on electricity most weeks and treat the gas engine as a safety net.

The case is weaker if you rarely tow and take few long trips. If your driving is local and you can charge at home, a normal EV is simpler and cheaper to maintain. It's also weaker if you can't charge at home, since an EREV with a flat battery is essentially a heavy hybrid, burning fuel to make electricity and giving up much of its advantage.

The question to ask before you order one

How many times a year do you actually tow far from home or drive beyond your battery range? If the honest answer is a handful of times, an EREV is a sensible hedge. If it's never, you're paying for a generator you'll rarely use, and a conventional EV or hybrid will likely cost less to buy and run.

Running Costs and Maintenance

Your fuel bill depends on how much of your driving happens on battery power. Charge at home overnight and cover a 40-mile commute on electricity, and you might go weeks without buying gas. Take a 500-mile trip and most of those miles will be powered by the engine, at efficiency that's decent but not extraordinary, since the engine is charging a battery rather than driving the wheels directly.

On maintenance, expect the worst of both worlds compared with a pure EV. There's still engine oil, filters, coolant and a fuel system to look after, plus the electric drivetrain. The upside is that the engine typically runs less often and at steadier loads, and regenerative braking still saves your brakes. Our guide to EV versus petrol running costs explains how these pieces add up over five years.

How You'd Actually Live With One

An EREV only makes sense if you plug it in. Charge overnight on a Level 2 home charger and you'd start each day with 100 to 150 miles of electric range, which covers the vast majority of daily driving. The engine would sit unused for weeks at a time.

Without home charging, the picture changes completely. You'd be running mostly on gasoline, hauling a large battery around for little benefit, and paying more for the privilege. If you can't install a charger where you park, a conventional hybrid will almost certainly serve you better.

Fast charging matters less on an EREV than on a pure EV, because you always have the generator as backup. Most EREVs support DC fast charging, but you can also simply keep driving and refuel at a gas station, which is the entire point of the design.

Towing: The Reason EREVs Exist

Towing is where these vehicles should shine. A pure electric truck pulling a heavy trailer can lose half its range or more, and fast chargers with pull-through bays that fit a truck and trailer remain rare. That combination turns a straightforward trip into a logistical problem.

An EREV handles it differently. The battery does the work until it's depleted, then the generator keeps the motors fed. You lose efficiency, and fuel economy while towing on generator power won't be impressive, but you don't have to plan the trip around charger locations or unhitch a trailer to reach a charging stall.

That said, wait for independent testing. Manufacturer range claims are made under favorable conditions, and the number that matters to a truck owner is how far it goes with 7,000 pounds behind it on a hot day.

What to Ask Before You Order One

These are new products with little track record, so a few direct questions are worth asking at the dealership:

  • What's the electric-only range, and what's the combined range with a full tank?
  • What fuel economy does it achieve once the battery is depleted and the generator is running?
  • Does it support DC fast charging, and at what maximum rate?
  • What's the warranty on the battery, the electric drive components and the engine, and are they covered separately?
  • What's the maintenance schedule for the engine, given it may run infrequently?
  • Can it power a home or job site, and what extra equipment does that need?

If a salesperson can't answer these clearly, get the answers in writing from the manufacturer's materials before you put down a deposit.

The Verdict

EREVs are a practical answer to real problems: towing range, cost, and patchy charging in rural areas. For truck buyers who want an electric drive without planning trips around chargers, they make a lot of sense, and it's easy to see why Ford, Ram and Scout are betting on them.

They're not a replacement for every EV, though. If you drive locally, charge at home and rarely tow, a battery-only EV remains simpler, cheaper to maintain and cheaper to run. As always, wait for independent range and efficiency testing before you put money down, because early figures are manufacturer estimates, and real-world towing numbers are what will decide whether these trucks live up to the promise.

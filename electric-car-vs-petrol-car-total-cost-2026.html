Electric Car vs Petrol Car in 2026: Complete Total Cost of Ownership Analysis

By Marcus Adeyemi·14 min read

The question people ask most about electric cars is whether they're cheaper to own. The real answer is "it depends," which is only useful if you know what it depends on. So instead of stopping there, this article runs a full, line-by-line cost comparison you can adapt to your own situation.

Cost comparisons that don't name specific cars don't tell you much. "EVs save on fuel but cost more up front" is true and nearly useless. What does help is something like this: a Tesla Model Y Premium AWD at $49,990 against a Toyota RAV4 Hybrid XLE Premium AWD at about $39,000, driven 15,000 miles a year in a mild US climate, charged at home at $0.16 per kWh, over five years. That's specific enough to actually add up.

That's the plan. And because the assumptions make a huge difference, they get their own section too.

The Vehicles We're Comparing

We picked the Tesla Model Y Premium AWD (the trim Tesla used to call Long Range AWD) and the Toyota RAV4 Hybrid XLE Premium AWD for a few reasons. Both are hugely popular, well-regarded SUVs aimed at buyers who want practical family transport. Both have been around long enough that there's real resale data. And the RAV4 Hybrid already gets excellent fuel economy, which makes this the toughest cost test for an EV, not the easiest. For 2026, every RAV4 is a hybrid, so this is the gas-powered RAV4 most shoppers will be looking at.

If you're comparing an EV with a gas-only car instead of a hybrid, the EV's fuel savings are much bigger. We'll show those numbers in the scenarios section. But EV versus hybrid is the matchup most buyers are weighing in 2026, so we'll start there.

Our main assumptions: 15,000 miles a year (close to the US average), home charging at $0.16 per kWh (around the national average), gas at $3.50 a gallon, a mild climate with no extreme cold or heat, and standard insurance from a major company. There's no federal EV tax credit in this comparison, because it ended for vehicles bought after September 30, 2025.

Step 1: Purchase Price

The Tesla Model Y Premium AWD lists at $49,990 before destination in 2026. Tesla sells at fixed prices, though it sometimes offers low-rate financing or other incentives, so check what's available when you buy. We'll use $49,990.

The Toyota RAV4 Hybrid XLE Premium starts at about $37,550, and adding all-wheel drive brings it to roughly $39,000 before destination. Depending on local supply, you may pay a little less or a little more. We'll use $39,000.

That makes the Tesla about $11,000 more expensive up front. Until late 2025, the $7,500 federal credit would have closed most of that gap for qualifying buyers. Now it's gone, so the whole difference lands on the buyer. Tesla's cheaper Model Y Standard AWD, at $41,990, narrows the gap to about $3,000, and we'll look at it in the scenarios.

For financing, we assumed $5,000 down on both, with the rest financed over 60 months at 6.5% APR, a typical rate for buyers with good credit. On the Tesla, financing $44,990 works out to about $880 a month. On the RAV4, financing $34,000 comes to about $665 a month. That's around $215 more each month for the Tesla, and it pays about $1,900 more in interest over the loan.

Step 2: Fuel Costs Over 5 Years

This is where the EV looks best, and also where the assumptions matter most, so let's be exact.

Tesla Model Y Premium AWD: we're assuming about 3.5 miles per kWh in mixed driving. At 15,000 miles a year, that's about 4,286 kWh. At $0.16 per kWh, it comes to roughly $686 a year, or about $3,430 over five years. Add about $200 a year for occasional road trip fast charging at around $0.45 per kWh, and the five-year total is about $4,430.

Toyota RAV4 Hybrid XLE Premium AWD: the EPA rates it at 41 mpg combined. At 15,000 miles a year, that's about 366 gallons. At $3.50 a gallon, it's roughly $1,280 a year, or about $6,400 over five years.

So over five years the Tesla saves about $1,970 on fuel. That's less than many EV fans expect, because the RAV4 Hybrid is so efficient. Against a gas-only compact SUV getting 28 mpg, the Tesla would save closer to $4,950 over five years.

Your electricity rate makes a big difference. On a special EV off-peak rate of $0.08 per kWh, which many utilities offer for overnight charging, the Tesla's home charging cost drops to about $343 a year, and the five-year savings against the RAV4 Hybrid climb to roughly $3,700. If you mostly charge at public fast chargers at $0.45 per kWh, the Tesla's fuel bill goes past $1,900 a year, more than the hybrid spends on gas. Whether you can charge at home is the single biggest factor in how much an EV saves you.

Step 3: Maintenance Costs Over 5 Years

EVs need far less routine maintenance than gas cars. The gap is smaller against a hybrid, because hybrids are also cheaper to maintain than regular gas cars.

Tesla Model Y over five years and 75,000 miles: no oil changes, much less brake wear thanks to regenerative braking (brakes get checked during tire rotations, and the first brake job often comes after 60,000 miles), tire rotations (about $60 to $72 each, around 10 times, so $600 to $720), cabin air filters ($60 to $100 total), and wiper blades ($60 to $100 total). Estimated routine maintenance: $750 to $1,000. With $500 set aside for minor repairs, the total is about $1,250 to $1,500.

Toyota RAV4 Hybrid over five years and 75,000 miles: oil changes (about $80 to $100 each, around 6 to 8 services), tire rotations ($600 to $720), cabin and engine air filters ($120 to $200), a possible 12V battery replacement around year five ($150 to $200), and a coolant service ($80 to $120). Estimated routine maintenance: $1,600 to $2,100. With $400 set aside for minor repairs, the total is about $2,000 to $2,500.

That's about $750 to $1,250 in maintenance savings for the Tesla over five years. Against a gas-only SUV, which needs more frequent oil changes and gets no help from regenerative braking, the gap would be much bigger.

Step 4: Insurance, the EV's Hidden Disadvantage

Many EV cost comparisons gloss over insurance or leave it out. It's a real and sizable disadvantage for EVs, especially Teslas, and it changes the five-year numbers a lot.

For a 35-year-old driver with a clean record in a moderately priced state, insuring a Tesla Model Y typically costs around $2,200 to $2,800 a year. That's more than most mainstream vehicles, for a few connected reasons. Teslas are expensive to repair. Their aluminum-heavy bodies and tightly integrated systems mean a minor fender bender can require panel replacement and sensor recalibration. Independent repair options are more limited, and parts costs are still above average, even as Tesla has expanded its repair network.

For the same driver, a 2026 Toyota RAV4 Hybrid usually costs about $1,400 to $1,800 a year to insure. Toyota's huge independent repair network, conventional construction, and strong reliability record all help keep premiums lower.

Over five years, at an average of $2,500 a year for the Tesla and $1,600 for the RAV4, the Tesla costs about $12,500 to insure and the RAV4 about $8,000. That $4,500 difference wipes out more than the Tesla's fuel and maintenance savings combined.

Insurance costs vary a lot by state, insurer, driving record, and coverage. In some high-cost states, Tesla premiums can reach $3,000 to $3,500 a year. In competitive, cheaper markets, the gap shrinks. Get real quotes before you finish your own comparison. National averages are a starting point, but your number could be very different.

Step 5: Depreciation, the Largest Cost and the Biggest Variable

For most cars, depreciation is the biggest cost over five years, and it's what makes or breaks the EV cost argument. It's also the most uncertain number here, because it depends on what the used car market looks like when you sell, and nobody can predict that reliably.

Here's what we know. The Model Y has lost value quickly in recent years, largely because Tesla cut new car prices repeatedly, which dragged down the value of cars already on the road. The end of the federal tax credit and Tesla's cheaper new trims add even more uncertainty. For our estimate, we're assuming the Model Y Premium AWD keeps about 50% of its price after five years, leaving it worth around $25,000. That means about $25,000 lost to depreciation.

The RAV4 Hybrid has one of the best resale records of any vehicle, thanks to steady demand and Toyota's reputation for reliability. We're assuming it keeps about 60% of its price after five years, leaving it worth around $23,400. That means about $15,600 lost to depreciation.

So the Tesla loses roughly $9,400 more to depreciation over five years. That gap is so large mainly because the Tesla costs about $11,000 more to begin with. It could grow if Tesla cuts prices again. It could shrink if used EV prices firm up as more people want them.

Step 6: Registration, EV Fees, and Additional Costs

Many states now charge EV owners an extra yearly registration fee to make up for the gas taxes they no longer pay toward road maintenance. These fees range from about $50 a year in some states to $250 or more in others. On average, EV owners pay roughly $100 to $150 a year more than hybrid or gas car owners, or about $500 to $750 over five years.

If you don't already have a home charger, include installation as part of the EV's cost. A Level 2 charger and installation usually runs $600 to $1,400, depending on your electrical panel and local labor rates. The federal home charger tax credit also ended for chargers installed after June 30, 2026, though some utilities still offer rebates. A hybrid owner pays nothing here.

A quick note on how the total works. What owning a car really costs you is the value it loses (depreciation), not the full purchase price, because you get some of that price back when you sell. So the total adds up depreciation, interest, fuel, maintenance, insurance, and fees, and leaves the purchase price out to avoid counting it twice.

In this scenario, the RAV4 Hybrid costs about $14,600 less to own over five years. That will surprise plenty of people who assume EVs always win on cost. The Tesla's fuel and maintenance savings are real, but they're swamped by its higher price, which also means bigger depreciation, plus higher insurance and EV-related fees.

How the Math Changes Under Different Assumptions

If you choose the Model Y Standard AWD instead: At $41,990, it's only about $3,000 more than the RAV4 Hybrid. With lower depreciation in dollars, less interest, and similar running costs, the five-year gap shrinks to somewhere around $8,000 to $9,000 in the RAV4's favor, depending on resale value and insurance.

If you're on a $0.08 off-peak electricity rate: The Tesla's five-year electricity cost falls to roughly $2,700 including some road trip charging, adding about $1,700 to its fuel savings. It still costs more to own, but the gap narrows.

If Tesla resale values hold up better: If the Model Y keeps 58% of its value after five years instead of 50%, it loses about $4,000 less to depreciation, and the overall gap narrows to around $10,500.

Tesla vs a gas-only compact SUV: A gas-only SUV at 28 mpg burns about $9,375 in fuel over five years, compared with the Tesla's $4,430, so the Tesla saves nearly $5,000 on fuel. Add bigger maintenance savings, and an EV starts to look much better, especially an affordable one like the Chevrolet Equinox EV or Nissan Leaf, whose purchase prices are close to a gas SUV's.

High-mileage driving (25,000 miles a year): At 25,000 miles a year, the Tesla's fuel savings against the RAV4 Hybrid grow to about $3,300 over five years instead of $1,970. The more you drive, the better the EV's case gets, though with a price gap this large, mileage alone rarely closes it against an efficient hybrid.

Who Wins, and the Honest Summary

In the scenario we've run, a Tesla Model Y Premium AWD against a RAV4 Hybrid XLE Premium AWD, 15,000 miles a year, home charging at average rates, and no federal tax credit, the RAV4 Hybrid is cheaper to own over five years by about $14,600. The EV's fuel and maintenance savings are real, but its higher purchase price, bigger depreciation, and higher insurance outweigh them.

An EV is most likely to win on total cost when you buy an affordable EV priced close to the gas or hybrid alternative, drive a lot of miles, have access to cheap overnight electricity, are comparing against a gas-only car instead of a hybrid, can find competitive insurance rates, or keep the car for seven years or more so the up-front premium is spread over more time. State or utility incentives can help too.

The hybrid is most likely to win when the EV costs a lot more up front, you'd rely mostly on public fast charging, you drive fewer than 12,000 miles a year, you live in a state with high EV registration fees, or you want the more predictable resale value of a proven model.

Our take: with the federal credit gone, pricier EVs have a harder time beating an efficient hybrid on cost alone. But affordable EVs with home charging can be very competitive, and the gap is often small enough that your preferences and driving habits should decide it, not the spreadsheet alone. The big shift of the last few years still stands: EVs are no longer a lot more expensive to run than gas cars once you count fuel and maintenance. Whether one saves you money depends heavily on which EV you buy and how you'll charge it.

The variable that matters most

Of everything in this analysis, two things change the answer most: how much more the EV costs up front, and whether you can charge at home. Home charging at $0.16 per kWh makes an EV cheap to run. Public charging at $0.45 per kWh can erase the fuel savings entirely. If you're considering an EV, compare it against a similarly priced gas or hybrid model, and get a quote for home charging installation before anything else.

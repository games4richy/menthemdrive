6 Best Cars for New Drivers in 2026: Safe, Affordable and Easy to Drive

By Marcus Adeyemi·13 min read

Picking a first car means ranking things differently than when you buy your third or fourth. Experienced drivers have the skills to handle cars with twitchier handling, bigger blind spots, or more power than a new driver should be dealing with. If you're buying for a new driver, or buying your own first car, those things matter a lot. Choosing for the wrong reasons, like sportiness, power, or sheer size, raises the risk of a crash and drives up insurance costs, which piles more financial pressure onto a new driver.

The cars below are ranked on four things that matter most for new drivers. The first is IIHS and NHTSA safety ratings, because new drivers crash more often and need a car that protects them when it happens. The second is insurance cost for young and new drivers, since insurance is often their biggest car expense, easily more than the monthly payment. The third is reliability for used examples in the $12,000 to $22,000 range, because most new driver budgets don't stretch to a new car. The fourth is how easy the car is to drive, including visibility, parking sensors, backup cameras, and predictable handling.

Why Safety Ratings Come Before Everything Else

According to the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, drivers aged 16 to 19 are nearly three times as likely per mile driven to be in a fatal crash as drivers 20 and older. That's not a criticism of teenagers. It reflects the real skill gap that comes with limited practice, along with specific risks of inexperience: spotting hazards late, overestimating what the car can do, and getting distracted more easily. Given that, putting a new driver in a car with poor crash protection is a real, measurable increase in risk compared with a car that protects well.

The IIHS Top Safety Pick+ award is one of the best signs of strong crash protection. It requires good results in a demanding set of crash tests, plus good headlights and effective crash avoidance technology. NHTSA's 5-Star Safety Ratings are the government's equivalent. The cars on this list have earned strong IIHS and NHTSA ratings in recent model years, but ratings vary by year and trim, so always check the exact car you're considering at iihs.org and nhtsa.gov. For a new driver, this isn't a nice extra. It's a must.

A quick word on what's actually required. Federal rules have required a backup camera on every new US vehicle since May 2018. Automatic emergency braking (AEB) became standard on nearly all new cars through a voluntary automaker agreement that was essentially complete by 2022, and a federal rule will require more advanced AEB on new vehicles by 2029. Lane departure warning, rear cross-traffic alert, and blind spot monitoring aren't required, but many of the cars below offer them as standard, even on base trims. For new drivers especially, blind spot monitoring and rear cross-traffic alert help catch the exact kinds of hazards inexperienced drivers most often miss.

The Insurance Cost Reality for New Drivers

An 18-year-old buying their own car insurance policy in 2026 can expect to pay roughly $3,800 to $6,500 a year, depending heavily on the state, driving record, and car. Adding a young driver to a parent's policy is usually cheaper, often an extra $1,200 to $2,500 a year, compared with a separate policy.

The car makes a big difference too. For a young driver, insurance on similar cars can vary by 15 to 35% between the cheapest and most expensive to insure. Cars that are costly to repair, stolen often, or linked to riskier driving cost more to insure. Cars with cheap, easy-to-find parts, simple repairs, and low theft rates cost less. The Honda Civic and Toyota Corolla regularly rank among the cheapest cars to insure for young drivers, while sports cars, trucks, and luxury cars tend to be among the most expensive.

1. Honda Civic: The Gold Standard for New Drivers (~$25,000 new, ~$14,000–$19,000 used)

The Honda Civic sits at the top of new driver lists because it's consistently good at everything, not because of one standout feature. It earns strong crash test results year after year, has some of the lowest insurance rates for young drivers, is reliable enough to be one of the most common high-mileage used cars around, and handles with a precision and predictability that helps new drivers build confidence without overwhelming them.

The Civic was redesigned for 2022, with big improvements to interior quality, technology, and safety. It has earned top IIHS awards and strong NHTSA ratings, and Honda Sensing, which includes adaptive cruise control, lane keeping assist, and automatic emergency braking, comes standard from the base model up. The base 2.0-liter engine makes 150 to 158 horsepower depending on the year, which is plenty for normal driving, cheap to maintain, and more forgiving of inexperienced driving than the more powerful versions. The Civic Hybrid, added for 2025, offers excellent fuel economy if the budget allows.

For used buyers, which is the more common route for a first car, Civic reliability has been excellent going back to 2016. A 2016 to 2021 Civic LX or Sport with 30,000 to 60,000 miles is one of the best used car values for a new driver. Low insurance, low maintenance costs, and proven reliability add up to a total cost that lets a new driver build savings instead of pouring everything into the car.

2. Toyota Corolla: Rock-Solid Reliability (~$23,000 new, ~$14,000–$19,000 used)

The Corolla's pitch is a little different from the Civic's. If the Civic is the better car to drive, with sharper steering and a more engaging feel, the Corolla is the safer bet for long-term ownership. Toyota's track record for avoiding both major mechanical failures and the electronic glitches that can plague some other brands as they age is among the best in the industry.

The current-generation Corolla hatchback arrived for 2019 and the sedan for 2020, with Toyota Safety Sense standard. That includes automatic emergency braking, lane departure alert with steering assist, automatic high beams, and adaptive cruise control, and newer model years have an upgraded version of the suite. The Corolla has earned strong IIHS and NHTSA ratings, though headlight performance can vary by trim, which sometimes affects its IIHS award level. Interior quality is a clear step up from older Corollas, which fixed their most common complaint.

The Corolla Hybrid deserves a special mention for new drivers. At around $25,000 new and roughly 50 mpg combined, it saves real money on fuel, which helps new drivers on tight budgets. Hybrids are also smooth and forgiving to drive, and their regenerative braking helps control speed on downhill stretches without heavy use of the brakes.

3. Mazda3: Best Driving Experience in the Segment (~$25,000 new, ~$16,000–$21,000 used)

Recommending the Mazda3 for new drivers might seem odd. Wouldn't a more engaging car be riskier for someone inexperienced? Not really. Mazda tunes its cars for predictability, feedback, and precision, not raw performance. The Mazda3's steering tells you more than the Civic's or Corolla's about what the car is doing and how close you are to its limits. That's safer than a car that tells you little and then surprises you when it runs out of grip.

The Mazda3 also has the nicest interior in its class by a clear margin, with materials, build quality, and design that feel upscale, not entry-level. For a new driver who'll spend years in the car, that makes daily driving more pleasant and is worth the small premium over a Civic or Corolla. The Mazda3 has earned top IIHS awards and strong NHTSA ratings in recent years, and Mazda's reliability has improved a lot since the 2010s, now ranking near the top in many reliability surveys.

4. Hyundai Elantra: Most Technology Per Dollar (~$22,500 new, ~$13,000–$18,000 used)

The current Hyundai Elantra packs in more technology, style, and features for the money than its rivals. Its styling stands out in a class full of conservative designs. Inside, many trims get a large dual-screen setup, with a digital instrument cluster and touchscreen side by side, at price points where rivals offer smaller or simpler screens. Hyundai SmartSense driver assistance comes standard, with a full set of features comparable to Honda Sensing and Toyota Safety Sense, and higher trims add more.

For a new car bought new, Hyundai's 10-year, 100,000-mile powertrain warranty offers real peace of mind for new drivers, who may not notice early warning signs of mechanical trouble and may drive harder on the car than experienced drivers. Keep in mind that the long powertrain coverage is for the original owner only, and used buyers get shorter coverage. The Elantra has earned strong safety ratings in recent years. Insurance can run a little higher than for a Civic or Corolla, mainly because of parts and repair costs on newer models, but it's still reasonable for the class.

5. Subaru Impreza: Best for Wet and Winter Weather States (~$23,500 new, ~$14,000–$19,000 used)

Every Subaru Impreza comes with Symmetrical All-Wheel Drive as standard, not as an option. For new drivers in places with lots of rain, snow, or ice, such as the Pacific Northwest, Mountain West, Northeast, and Midwest, AWD adds a real safety margin on slippery roads, where new drivers are less confident than experienced ones. AWD won't stop a car from sliding if it's going too fast, but it does cut down on those scary moments on slick roads during normal driving.

The current Impreza, redesigned for 2024, is sold as a hatchback only, while earlier generations also offered a sedan. Older Imprezas from the 2017 to 2023 generation are widely available used and have earned strong safety ratings, and many come with EyeSight, Subaru's driver assistance suite, which includes pre-collision braking, adaptive cruise control, and lane departure warning. EyeSight is especially well regarded for how well it avoids front-end collisions in independent testing. When shopping used, check that the specific car has EyeSight, since not every older trim does.

6. Volkswagen Jetta: European Feel on a Realistic Budget (~$22,500 new, ~$13,000–$18,000 used)

The Jetta drives differently from its Japanese rivals. It feels a little heavier and more planted, with steering that some new drivers find more reassuring than the lighter feel of Japanese cars. The interior is nicer than average for the price. Volkswagen's IQ.DRIVE driver assistance, standard or available depending on the year and trim, includes adaptive cruise control with stop-and-go, lane assist, forward collision warning with emergency braking, and rear traffic alert.

There's an important reliability caveat. Volkswagen's long-term reliability generally trails Toyota and Honda in most surveys, and Jettas past 80,000 miles are more likely to need unexpected repairs than a Civic or Corolla. If a new driver plans to keep the car five years or more and wants to avoid expensive surprises, the Civic and Corolla are safer bets. If driving feel and interior quality matter more to you and you're comfortable with the chance of higher maintenance costs, the Jetta is a reasonable choice at its price.

What New Drivers Should Specifically Avoid

High-horsepower cars, however "safe" they seem otherwise. A sports car or muscle car with 300-plus horsepower demands car control that new drivers don't have yet. New drivers crash these cars much more often than ordinary sedans and hatchbacks, which is exactly why insurance on them costs so much more for young drivers. Save the sports car for after you've got several years of driving under your belt.

Large trucks and big SUVs, unless you really need the space or towing. They have big blind spots, need more room to park, and handle very differently from sedans and small SUVs in ways that take experience to manage. Their higher center of gravity raises rollover risk. And the feeling many new drivers have, that they'll be safe because they're in something big, isn't fully backed by the data. Size helps in some crashes, but many large trucks and body-on-frame SUVs don't score as well in some crash tests as modern small cars, and they take longer to stop.

Old cars with weak safety ratings, bought to save money. A $5,000 car from 2008, with no automatic emergency braking, no backup camera, and crash ratings from an era of lower standards, puts a new driver at noticeably higher risk than a $14,000 car from 2018 with modern safety tech. The money you save up front isn't worth the added risk. Budget at least $12,000 to $14,000 for a new driver's first car, and put safety ratings and reliability ahead of finding the cheapest possible option.

Buying Advice for Parents Choosing Their Child's First Car

The most common mistake parents make is choosing a car they'd like for themselves instead of one that suits their teenager's needs and risks. A hand-me-down luxury car with shaky reliability past 80,000 miles isn't a better choice than a base Honda Civic just because of the badge. And a performance sedan that makes a teen feel confident and capable isn't a better choice than an ordinary compact just because the teen prefers it.

Base the decision on a few objective things. Check current IIHS and NHTSA safety ratings for the exact model year at iihs.org and nhtsa.gov. Get real insurance quotes with the young driver included, not estimates, because the differences between cars can be large. And look up reliability data for the specific year and mileage you're shopping, using sources like Consumer Reports, Edmunds, and RepairPal.

Insurance telematics programs can offer both safety monitoring and discounts, often 10 to 30%, for new drivers who show safe habits. Programs like Progressive Snapshot and State Farm Drive Safe & Save are worth a look. The discounts can be significant, and the driving data gives you an objective picture of how your teen actually drives, without having to take their word for it.

The single most important feature to prioritize

Automatic emergency braking with pedestrian detection. Of all the driver assistance technologies out there, AEB with pedestrian detection has the strongest evidence for reducing real-world crashes and how severe they are. It's standard on virtually all new cars in 2026, but on used cars, it's generally found on models from around 2016 onward, and often only on mid and upper trims before about 2020. Make AEB a must-have in your used car search. It's the technology that most directly makes up for the specific risks new and young drivers face.

Lowering Insurance Costs for New Drivers

New and young drivers usually pay more for insurance than anyone else, often $3,500 to $6,500 a year on their own policy. Several strategies can bring that down without changing the car. Adding the new driver to a parent's existing policy instead of buying a separate one typically saves $1,000 to $2,500 a year for the same coverage. Being listed as an occasional driver on an older, paid-off car on the parent's policy can cut costs further, as long as that honestly reflects how the car is used.

Good student discounts, usually 5 to 15%, are available from most major insurers for full-time students with a B average or better. Ask about it directly instead of assuming it's applied. Many insurers also give discounts for completing driver's education or defensive driving courses beyond the minimum licensing requirements. Telematics programs from Progressive (Snapshot), State Farm (Drive Safe & Save), and others offer discounts, often 10 to 30%, for safe driving tracked through a phone app or plug-in device. They're a great option for careful new drivers, and many young people drive more carefully knowing they're being monitored.

The car itself can change insurance costs by roughly 15 to 35% between the cheapest and most expensive options for the same driver. The Honda Civic and Toyota Corolla consistently have some of the lowest insurance costs for young drivers among similar cars. Sporty versions of compact cars, like the Civic Si, VW GTI, and Subaru WRX, can cost 20 to 45% more to insure than their base versions for the same driver, which can add up to thousands of dollars a year. For a new driver, the base engine in a safe, capable car is the right choice.

Summary: The One Vehicle to Start With

If we had to pick one car for the widest range of new drivers, it would be a used Honda Civic LX from about 2020 to 2023, bought for roughly $15,000 to $18,000 with 25,000 to 50,000 miles, after a pre-purchase inspection and a clean vehicle history report. That combination gets you Honda Sensing safety tech, including automatic emergency braking and lane keeping, Honda's excellent long-term reliability, some of the lowest insurance costs for new drivers, and a driving feel that builds real confidence without encouraging overconfidence. It's not an exciting pick, and that's exactly why it's the right one.

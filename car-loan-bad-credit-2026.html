How to Get a Car Loan With Bad Credit in 2026: Real Options That Work Without Getting Scammed

By Marcus Adeyemi·13 min read

You can get a car loan with bad credit in 2026, but the details matter. Online advice tends to either panic ("bad credit means no loan") or brush it off ("anyone can get a car loan!"). The truth is in between. Legitimate lenders do make loans to people with bad credit, those loans cost a lot more than good-credit loans, and the difference between going in blind and going in prepared can easily reach $5,000 to $10,000 over the life of the loan.

One warning before we start. The businesses that serve people with bad credit include some of the most exploitative lending in consumer finance. Some buy-here-pay-here lots, predatory online lenders, and dealer financing setups are designed to squeeze as much interest as possible out of borrowers who feel they have nowhere else to go. This article aims to give you enough information to use legitimate lenders and steer clear of the predatory ones.

Here's where you stand, based on your credit score.

What Your Credit Score Means for Your Rate

FICO scores run from 300 to 850, and auto lenders sort borrowers into tiers, each with its own typical range of rates. Knowing your tier tells you what to expect and whether a rate you're offered is fair for your credit or a rip-off.

The tiers are a rough guide, and rates vary quite a bit within each one depending on the lender, your local market, the size of the loan compared with the car's value, your down payment, and your debt-to-income ratio. Take two borrowers with a 580 score. One has steady work, three years at the same address, $5,000 down, and no recent late payments. The other has recent late payments and nothing down. The first will get a noticeably better rate, even though both are in the same tier.

Know your score before you apply. Many credit cards, including Discover and Capital One, show your score for free every month, and many banks now show a credit score in online banking. You can also get your full credit reports from all three bureaus for free at AnnualCreditReport.com. The reports don't include a score, but they show exactly what lenders will see. Know your actual number, not just that it's "bad." A 560, a 580, and a 620 are all considered bad credit, but lenders treat them quite differently.

On the same $20,000 car, the monthly payment for a deep-subprime borrower can be $150 to $260 more than for a super-prime borrower. Over a 60-month loan, that's roughly $9,000 to $15,500, almost the price of another car. That's a strong reason to use every strategy available to get the best rate you can, and to think hard about whether a car is truly affordable once you count the total interest at your credit tier.

Where to Get Pre-Approved: The Right Sequence

The most important thing you can do before setting foot in a dealership is get pre-approved for a loan from somewhere other than the dealer. Pre-approval gives you bargaining power, a benchmark to compare the dealer's financing against, and the peace of mind of knowing you already have a loan lined up. Here's the order we recommend.

Step 1: Credit Unions, Your Most Powerful Tool

Credit unions are owned by their members, and they work differently from banks in ways that really help borrowers with bad credit. Banks answer to shareholders and aim to maximize profit. Credit unions exist to serve their members. In practice, that often means lower auto loan rates, commonly a point or more below comparable bank rates, and more flexible approval decisions that look at your whole financial situation, not just your score.

That flexibility is especially valuable if your credit is poor. A bank's automated system might reject a 560 score without a person ever looking at it. A credit union loan officer who can see your job history and steady paychecks, and understands why you have a late payment from 18 months ago, maybe from a disputed medical bill, can make a decision a computer wouldn't. Most borrowers don't know to look for that human touch, but it can make a real difference.

If you already belong to a credit union, apply there first, before anything else. If you don't, several credit unions are open to almost anyone. Navy Federal Credit Union serves military members, veterans, and their families. PenFed Credit Union has fairly open membership. Alliant Credit Union lets you join by making a small donation to a partner charity. Many state employee, teacher, and employer credit unions also have simple eligibility rules. Check whether you or a family member qualifies anywhere before assuming banks are your only option.

When applying to a credit union with bad credit, go in person if you can. The relationship-based lending that makes credit unions so useful works better face to face, where you can talk to a loan officer, explain your credit history, and show the stability an online form can't capture. Bring two recent pay stubs, your latest bank statements, a copy of your credit report so they can see you understand your situation, and ideally a short letter explaining any major negatives, like late payments or collections, what caused them, and what you've done to fix them.

Step 2: Online Lenders That Serve Subprime Borrowers

Several online lenders specialize in borrowers with scores below 600 and run legitimate lending programs for this market. They look at more than your score, including how long you've been at your job, your income, and your debt-to-income ratio.

Capital One's Auto Navigator lets you pre-qualify without a hard credit inquiry, see an estimated rate, and browse dealer inventory that accepts Capital One financing, all before visiting a dealer. Pre-qualification uses a soft credit check that won't affect your score. If you go ahead with the actual loan, that's a hard inquiry. Capital One is often willing to lend to borrowers with scores between about 500 and 650 when other mainstream lenders say no.

Carvana and CarMax both offer their own financing, which can work for borrowers with lower scores, though rates are usually higher than a credit union's. Their advantage is a simple, clear pre-approval process that takes dealer financing games out of the picture. If Carvana pre-approves you at 18% and your credit union won't go below 21%, Carvana's offer is the better deal, even though neither is cheap.

Other lenders, such as RoadLoans, focus on subprime auto loans for purchases, while services like Autopay and LendingClub are mainly known for refinancing, which becomes useful later once your credit improves. Whichever lenders you use, try to do all your loan applications within a short window. FICO scoring models treat multiple auto loan inquiries within a set period, 14 days in older versions and up to 45 days in newer ones, as a single inquiry. Keep your rate shopping within 14 days to be safe instead of spreading applications over months.

Step 3: Dealer Financing, Used Strategically, Not by Default

Most people with bad credit head straight to a dealership and accept whatever financing the dealer arranges, because it feels easiest. It's usually the most expensive way to do it.

Dealer financing often includes something called "dealer reserve." The dealer sends your application to a group of lenders, sometimes 10 to 20 at once, gets back approvals with rates, and then quotes you a rate that includes a markup over what the lender actually offered. That markup is commonly up to about 2 to 2.5 percentage points and can be more in some cases. On a $15,000 loan, 22% instead of 18% costs about $2,000 extra over 60 months, and you pay it in every monthly payment without ever seeing it.

Dealer financing isn't always a rip-off. Competition among lenders can sometimes produce a good rate, and some dealers limit their markups to keep customers happy. But you can't judge the dealer's rate without something to compare it to, which is why getting pre-approved first is so important. If the dealer beats your pre-approval, great, take it. If not, you have a concrete alternative to point to, and you can use your own financing no matter what the dealer would prefer.

Strategies to Materially Improve Your Terms

The Down Payment Strategy: More Than You Think

A bigger down payment helps in three ways that build on each other. First, it shrinks the loan, so you pay less interest at any rate. Second, it lowers your loan-to-value ratio (LTV), how much you borrow compared with what the car is worth, which lenders use to judge risk. An LTV under about 80% is much less risky for a lender, and some will offer a better rate for it even at the same credit score. Third, a solid down payment shows you're financially responsible, which can help when a credit union loan officer is reviewing your application personally.

On a $15,000 car, putting $4,000 down instead of $1,000 means borrowing $3,000 less. At 18% over 60 months, that saves about $1,570 in interest. If the better LTV also gets you a rate a point or two lower, you could save another $400 to $800. All told, that extra $3,000 down could cut your total cost by roughly $2,000 or more over the loan, a better return than most savings accounts or investments will give you.

Loan Term: The Hidden Trap That Costs Thousands

The most tempting trap in bad-credit car loans is the long loan term. A 72 or 84-month loan makes the monthly payment much lower, which feels like a relief when money's already tight, but it makes the total interest you pay much higher.

Take a $15,000 loan at 18% APR. Here's the monthly payment and total interest at different lengths:

  • 48 months: about $441 a month, about $6,150 in interest.
  • 60 months: about $381 a month, about $7,850 in interest.
  • 72 months: about $342 a month, about $9,630 in interest.
  • 84 months: about $315 a month, about $11,480 in interest.

The 84-month loan is only about $125 a month cheaper than the 48-month loan, but it costs roughly $5,300 more in interest. The "affordable" long loan is the most expensive choice by far.

Long loans also make it more likely you'll owe more than the car is worth. A $15,000 car keeps losing value while you pay it off. Six years into a subprime loan, you might still owe several thousand dollars on a car worth less than that, and one that's getting close to the end of its reliable life. If it's totaled or needs a major repair while you're underwater, you can end up in a real financial mess. Choose the shortest loan term your budget can honestly handle, not the one that feels most comfortable today.

Adding a Co-Signer: When It Makes Sense and What It Means

If a family member or partner with good credit agrees to co-sign, the lender considers both of your credit histories. A co-signer with a 720 score can turn a 16% subprime offer into something much closer to a near-prime rate on the same car, which can save you a lot each month and thousands over the loan.

Both of you need to understand the legal and personal consequences before going ahead. A co-signer is just as legally responsible for the loan as you are. If you miss a payment, the lender can go after the co-signer, and the late payment shows up on both of your credit reports. If you stop paying altogether, the co-signer's credit takes the same hit as yours. The loan also counts toward the co-signer's debts, which can make it harder for them to get their own mortgage or car loan.

So only co-sign when there's real trust, full openness about finances, and a frank conversation about what happens if you can't make a payment. Co-signing that goes wrong can seriously damage relationships as well as credit scores. If your relationship can't handle an honest conversation about those risks, it probably shouldn't involve co-signing.

What to Avoid in Bad Credit Auto Financing

Some financing setups aimed at people with bad credit are genuinely predatory. Treat them as things to avoid, not as imperfect but acceptable options.

Yo-yo financing is when a dealer lets you drive the car home while your financing is supposedly "being finalized," then calls days or weeks later to say the deal "fell through" and you need to sign a new one with worse terms. By then you've been driving the car, your family may be attached to it, and the pressure to just accept the worse deal is intense. Getting out cleanly can be legally messy. To protect yourself, don't take the car until your financing is fully approved and signed in writing. A trustworthy dealer will tell you clearly when financing is approved, not "pending."

Extended warranty upsells in the finance office are another way high-interest dealers extract more money from borrowers already paying steep rates. Finance managers are trained to pitch these as the careful, responsible choice, which lands especially well with people who've had money troubles and want to avoid more surprises. But the markups at subprime dealerships can be huge. Say no to add-ons in the finance office. If you want an extended warranty, you can usually buy one later, ideally from the manufacturer, when you can compare prices without pressure.

Buy-Here-Pay-Here: The Full Truth

Buy-here-pay-here (BHPH) dealers advertise "no credit check" or "everyone's approved" because they lend you the money themselves instead of going through a bank. Their business model often depends on very high interest rates, frequently above 20% and sometimes near the legal limits, inflated car prices, and GPS devices or starter interrupters that let them track the car or disable it if you miss a payment, sometimes very quickly.

Cars at BHPH lots tend to be older, higher-mileage, and more likely to have mechanical problems than similar cars at regular dealers, and they're often priced above market value to maximize profit. Weekly or biweekly payment schedules, combined with the ability to disable the car remotely, give the dealer a lot of power and leave you very exposed.

Here's the key point: many BHPH dealers don't report your payments to the credit bureaus. So even if you pay on time for two years, your credit score may not improve at all, and poor credit is probably what led you there in the first place. You pay high rates, take on a big repossession risk, and may get no credit-building benefit. If you're considering one, ask directly whether they report to all three credit bureaus. Treat BHPH as a last resort for the most extreme situations, not a plan. If your credit is too damaged for the legitimate lenders covered here, spending 6 to 12 months rebuilding your credit (see below) is usually the smarter move.

Using This Loan to Rebuild Your Credit

An auto loan you pay on time every month is one of the best ways to rebuild bad credit. Payment history makes up 35% of your FICO score, and 12 to 24 months of on-time car payments can go a long way toward repairing a score damaged by past money problems. An auto loan also adds to your credit mix, 10% of your FICO score, which helps if most of your credit is credit cards.

After 12 months of on-time payments, check your score. If it has improved meaningfully, and gains of 40 to 60 points are common for borrowers who pay on time and keep other debts in check, look into refinancing. Refinancing a subprime car loan after a year of clean payments can sometimes cut your rate by several percentage points, which can save a lot over the rest of the loan. Lenders and marketplaces that focus on auto refinancing, such as Autopay, OpenRoad Lending, and myAutoloan, are worth comparing once your credit has improved.

A 12-Month Credit Improvement Plan Before Buying

If you can wait at all, spending 6 to 12 months improving your credit before applying for a car loan is the single most valuable thing you can do. The rate gap between a 560 and a 620 score can be several percentage points, often worth $3,000 to $6,000 on a typical car loan. Here's how to move from around 560 to 620 in 6 to 12 months:

  • Pay every account on time. Don't miss a single payment.
  • Get your credit card balances below 30% of each card's limit. How much of your available credit you use makes up about 30% of your FICO score and responds quickly when you pay balances down.
  • Dispute real errors on your credit reports. Mistakes are more common than most people think. A major Federal Trade Commission study found that about one in four consumers had an error on at least one of their credit reports.

If you can take even six months to rebuild your credit before buying, the savings over the life of the loan will usually far outweigh the hassle of waiting. Saving $5,000 in interest over 60 months is real money, enough to cover several months of other transportation while your credit improves.

How to Lower Your Car Insurance in 2026: 12 Ways to Cut Your Premium

By Marcus Adeyemi·September 13, 2026·12 min read

Car insurance has become one of the biggest line items in owning a car. Premiums rose steeply between 2022 and 2024, and while increases have slowed since, most drivers are still paying far more than they were a few years ago. Depending on which survey you read, full coverage now averages somewhere north of $2,000 a year, and plenty of drivers pay well beyond that.

The good news is that insurance is one of the few car costs you can cut this week, without selling anything or changing how you drive. Here are twelve things that actually work, roughly in order of how much they're worth, plus the one cut you should never make.

1. Shop Around Every Renewal

This is the big one. Insurers change their pricing constantly, and the company that was cheapest for you three years ago may now be among the most expensive. Loyalty rarely pays, and some insurers quietly raise renewal prices for customers who never leave.

Get quotes from at least five companies, including a couple you haven't heard much about. Use identical coverage limits and deductibles for each quote, or you'll be comparing different products. Do this once a year, or whenever your renewal arrives with an increase. It's an hour of work that regularly saves hundreds of dollars.

2. Raise Your Deductible, If You Have Savings

Moving from a $500 to a $1,000 deductible typically cuts comprehensive and collision premiums by around 10 to 20%. The trade-off is that you pay the first $1,000 of any at-fault claim.

Only do this if you could comfortably cover that amount tomorrow. If a $1,000 bill would send you to a credit card, keep the lower deductible. The savings aren't worth being unable to fix your car.

3. Bundle Home or Renters Insurance

Most insurers offer 10 to 25% off when you buy auto and home, condo or renters coverage together. Renters insurance is cheap on its own, and bundling it can sometimes save more on the car policy than the renters policy costs.

Do check the bundled total against buying each separately from different companies. Bundling usually wins, but not always.

4. Ask for Every Discount by Name

Insurers don't always apply discounts automatically. Go through the list with an agent and ask specifically about:

  • Safe driver or claim-free discounts
  • Low annual mileage
  • Good student, for teens and college students with a B average or better
  • Defensive driving or driver education courses
  • Paperless billing and automatic payments
  • Paying the policy in full
  • Anti-theft devices and certain safety features
  • Occupation or alumni group affiliations
  • Multi-car policies

Individually these are small, often 3 to 10% each. Stacked, they add up.

5. Pay in Full Instead of Monthly

Many insurers add a service fee to monthly payments, and some offer an additional discount for paying the whole term at once. Between the two, paying in full can save roughly 5 to 10% compared with the monthly plan. If you can manage the lump sum, it's one of the easiest savings available.

6. Try a Telematics Program (With Eyes Open)

Usage-based programs track how you drive through an app or a plug-in device, looking at hard braking, fast acceleration, phone handling, mileage and time of day. Safe drivers can save 10 to 30%.

Two caveats. First, with some insurers, poor scores can raise your rate rather than just failing to lower it, so ask directly whether the program can increase your premium. Second, if you drive a lot at night or in heavy traffic, you may score worse than you expect through no fault of your own. Read the terms before enrolling.

7. Update Your Annual Mileage

Many people's mileage estimate is years out of date, often set when they had a longer commute. If you now work from home part of the week or drive less than you used to, tell your insurer. Low-mileage drivers pay less, and some companies offer pay-per-mile policies that suit people driving under about 7,500 miles a year.

8. Improve Your Credit, Where It's Allowed

In most states, insurers use a credit-based insurance score, and it can have a surprisingly large effect on your premium. A few states, including California, Hawaii and Massachusetts, restrict or ban the practice, and Michigan limits it.

If you live somewhere it's allowed, the same habits that raise your credit score will lower your insurance over time: pay on time, keep card balances well below their limits, and fix errors on your reports. This is slow, but it compounds with other benefits, like better loan rates, which we cover in our bad credit car loan guide.

9. Reconsider Comprehensive and Collision on an Older Car

Comprehensive and collision only ever pay out up to the car's value, minus your deductible. On an old car, that ceiling gets low.

A common rule of thumb: if the annual premium for comprehensive and collision is more than about 10% of what the car is worth, consider dropping it. On a car worth $3,000 with a $1,000 deductible, the most you'd collect in a total loss is $2,000, so paying $400 a year for that protection is questionable. Look up your car's value on Kelley Blue Book or Edmunds before deciding, and never drop it on a financed car, because your lender requires it.

10. Factor Insurance Into Which Car You Buy

Two cars at the same price can cost very different amounts to insure. Repair costs, theft rates, crash test results and typical driver profiles all feed into the price. Sporty versions are especially expensive, sometimes 20 to 45% more than the base model of the same car.

Get insurance quotes before you buy, not after. It takes minutes with a VIN or even just the model and trim, and it can change which version you choose. This matters most for households with young drivers, as our guide to cars for new drivers explains.

11. Clean Up Coverage You're Paying for Twice

Check whether you're paying for extras you already have. Roadside assistance may come with your car's warranty, a credit card or an auto club membership. Rental reimbursement may be unnecessary if you have a second car. Some drivers also carry medical coverages that overlap heavily with their health insurance, though the right answer here depends on your state and your health plan.

Cancel the genuine duplicates, and leave the rest alone.

12. Tell Your Insurer About Life Changes

Several ordinary events lower rates, but only if you report them. Moving to an area with fewer claims, getting married, turning 25, retiring, a teen driver going away to college without a car, or finishing a policy period with no claims can all reduce what you pay. Old violations also age out, typically after three to five years, and a rate that reflects a ticket you got four years ago may be ready to drop.

The one cut you should never make

Don't reduce your liability limits to the state minimum to save money. Minimums are very low in many states, often far less than the cost of a serious injury claim or a newer car you might hit. If you cause an accident that exceeds your coverage, you're personally responsible for the rest. Most experts suggest at least $100,000 per person and $300,000 per accident in bodily injury coverage. Raising your deductible saves money safely; cutting liability does not.

Why Insurance Got So Expensive

It helps to know that this isn't personal. Premiums rose because claims got more expensive. Cars now carry cameras, radar and sensors in bumpers and windshields, so even minor collisions require calibration and pricier parts. Repair labor costs more, cars are worth more, and severe weather has driven up comprehensive claims in many states.

That's also why shopping around matters more than it used to. Insurers have reacted to those costs at different speeds and in different states, so the spread between the cheapest and most expensive quote for the same driver is often very wide.

How to Compare Quotes Properly

Line up the same numbers on every quote: bodily injury and property damage liability limits, comprehensive and collision deductibles, uninsured motorist coverage, and any medical coverage. A cheaper quote with lower limits isn't cheaper, it's less insurance.

Check the company as well as the price. Look at complaint records, which your state insurance department publishes, and read about claims handling rather than just marketing. Your state's insurance department website is also the right place for local rules and consumer guides, and the National Association of Insurance Commissioners can point you to yours.

Know What You're Actually Buying

It's hard to compare quotes without knowing what each part does, so here's the short version.

Liability pays for injuries and damage you cause to others. It doesn't repair your car. This is the coverage you should never skimp on.

Collision repairs your car after an accident you cause. Comprehensive covers things that aren't collisions: theft, hail, flood, fire, falling branches and cracked windshields.

Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage protects you when the other driver has no insurance or not enough. Given how many drivers are uninsured in some states, this is usually worth keeping.

Medical payments or personal injury protection covers medical bills after a crash, and the rules vary a lot by state.

When you compare quotes, match all of these line by line. A cheaper premium with weaker limits isn't a saving, it's a smaller safety net.

If You Have a Teen Driver

Adding a teenager is the single biggest premium increase most households ever see, often doubling the bill. A few things genuinely help.

Add them to your existing policy rather than buying a separate one. Make sure the good student discount is applied, since it's usually worth 5 to 15%. Enroll in a telematics program, because young drivers who know they're being monitored often post better scores than expected. And assign them to the cheapest car in the household rather than the newest one, which insurers price accordingly. Our guide to the best cars for new drivers covers which models keep those costs down.

After a Ticket, Claim or Rate Hike

A surcharge after an at-fault accident or a ticket usually lasts three to five years, but it doesn't have to stay with the same insurer. Because companies weigh violations differently, the one that penalizes you most heavily is rarely the cheapest option afterward. Shop specifically after any incident, and again when the violation passes its anniversary.

If your rate jumps at renewal with no claims or tickets on your record, ask the insurer to explain it in writing. Rate increases often reflect broader trends in your ZIP code rather than anything you did, and that's precisely the situation where switching companies pays off.

Switching Mid-Policy Is Allowed

You don't have to wait for your renewal date. If you find a better rate, you can switch at any time, and your current insurer must refund the unused portion of your premium. Some companies charge a small cancellation fee, so check first, but it's rarely enough to offset real savings.

Two rules when switching. Never let the old policy lapse before the new one starts, because even a short gap in coverage raises your future rates and can be illegal. And tell your lender or leasing company about the new policy, since they'll require proof of coverage.

The Bottom Line

If you do only two things this month, get five fresh quotes with identical coverage and ask your current insurer to list every discount you're not receiving. Those two steps take about an hour and produce most of the savings available to the average driver.

After that, the bigger levers are structural: the deductible you can afford, the car you choose to insure, and the credit profile you build over time. None of it requires giving up the protection that makes insurance worth having in the first place.
